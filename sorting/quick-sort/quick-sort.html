Pick a Pivot · Partition · Recurse

Quick Sort

Pick one value, shove everything smaller to its left and bigger to its right, then sort each side the same way — fast, in place, and everywhere in practice.

Overview Playground

The idea, in one picture

Pick any value from the array — call it the pivot. Now partition: rearrange so everything smaller than the pivot ends up to its left and everything larger to its right. After that one pass, the pivot is in its final sorted position, even though neither side is sorted yet. Then do the same thing to the left side and the right side, all the way down. When every piece is size 1, the whole array is sorted.

Divide and conquer, in place. Like merge sort, quick sort splits the problem in half and recurses — but it does the work before recursing (partitioning) and needs no extra array. That's why it's usually the fastest general-purpose sort in practice.

Going deeper

cost, the worst case, and the fix
CaseWhenTime
Best / AveragePivot splits roughly in halfO(n log n)
WorstPivot is always the min/max (e.g. sorted input, last-element pivot)O(n²)
SpaceIn place (recursion stack)O(log n)

The pivot choice is everything. If each pivot lands near the middle, the array halves each level — log n levels × O(n) work = O(n log n). But a consistently bad pivot (like always taking the last element on already-sorted data) makes one side empty every time — n levels, O(n²). Real implementations dodge this by choosing the pivot as the median of three samples or a random element, making the worst case astronomically unlikely.

Quick vs merge. Quick sort is in-place and usually faster in practice (better cache behaviour), but unstable and O(n²) in the worst case. Merge sort guarantees O(n log n) and is stable, but needs O(n) extra space. Both beat the O(n²) simple sorts.
