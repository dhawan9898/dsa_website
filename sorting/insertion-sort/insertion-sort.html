Insertion Sort

Exactly how you sort a hand of playing cards: pick up the next card and slide it back into its right place among the ones you've already sorted.

Overview Playground

The idea, in one picture

Hold a few sorted cards in your left hand. Pick up the next card with your right. Slide it leftwards past every card that's bigger, and drop it into the gap. Your left hand is still sorted — just one card bigger. Repeat until there are no cards left to pick up.

Insertion sort is that, on an array. It grows a sorted region on the left. Each step takes the first unsorted value (the "key"), walks it left past everything larger — shifting those up to make room — and drops it into place.

It shifts, it doesn't swap. Rather than swapping pairs, it copies bigger values one slot to the right to open a gap, then sets the gap to the key once. And it stops walking the moment it meets something not bigger — so on nearly-sorted data each key barely moves, giving near-O(n) speed.

cost and character
CaseWhenTime
BestAlready (nearly) sortedO(n)
Average / WorstRandom / reversedO(n²)
SpaceIn placeO(1)

The useful simple sort. It's stable, in-place, and genuinely fast when the data is almost sorted or small — which is why real libraries switch to insertion sort for tiny subarrays inside quick sort and merge sort. Its gap-based generalization is shell sort. For large random data, though, the O(n²) shifts still make an O(n log n) sort the better default.
