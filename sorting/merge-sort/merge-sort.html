Merge Sort

Split the array in half, sort each half, then merge the two sorted halves together — guaranteed O(n log n), every time.

Overview Playground

The idea, in one picture

Merge sort is the purest divide and conquer. Splitting an array in half is trivial. Sorting a one-element piece is free — it's already sorted. The clever part is merging: given two already sorted halves, you can zip them into one sorted whole in a single linear pass — just keep taking the smaller of the two front elements.

So the whole algorithm is: split until pieces are size 1, then merge pairs of pieces back up, doubling the sorted size each level, until the whole array is one sorted run.

The merge is the engine. Two sorted lists merge in O(n) because each list only moves forward — a pointer at the front of each, take the smaller, advance it. No value is ever compared twice. That linear merge, done at every level of the split, is what gives merge sort its speed.

Guaranteed and stable. Unlike quick sort, merge sort is O(n log n) in every case — there's no bad pivot to trip it up — and it's stable, which matters when you sort records by one field and want ties to keep their previous order. Its cost is the O(n) extra array the merge needs. It's the go-to for sorting linked lists (where splitting and merging is natural and needs no extra array) and for external sorts of data too big for memory. Its two-halves-then-merge shape is a cousin of heap sort's guarantee and quick sort's divide-and-conquer.
