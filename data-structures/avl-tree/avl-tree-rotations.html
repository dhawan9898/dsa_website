Rotations

A rotation re-hangs three pointers to lift a child above its parent — rebalancing the tree while keeping every key in sorted order.

The four cases

When a node's balance factor hits ±2, where the offending insert happened decides which of four shapes you're in — and each has a fixed fix:

LL — left subtree's left is too tall → one right rotation.
RR — right subtree's right is too tall → one left rotation.
LR — left subtree's right is too tall → rotate the child left, then the node right.
RL — right subtree's left is too tall → rotate the child right, then the node left.

LL and RR are mirror images of each other, and so are LR and RL — so there are really just two mechanics to learn: a single rotation, and a double rotation (which is just two singles).

Single rotation (the RR case)

one left rotation lifts the right child up

Here node 10 is right-heavy by 2, and the heaviness continues to the right (RR). A single left rotation at 10 pulls its right child, 20, up to be the new subtree root; 10 drops to become 20's left child. The one subtly is the middle subtree (15): it was on 20's left, but that spot now belongs to 10 — so 15 re-hangs as 10's right child. It still sits between 10 and 20, so the ordering is untouched.

 

Why it stays sorted. A rotation only ever moves subtrees to positions the BST rule still allows — it re-hangs three links (the pivot, its child, and one middle subtree) and nothing else. The keys' left < node < right relationship holds before and after. LL is the exact mirror: a single right rotation.

Double rotation (the LR case)

a zig-zag needs two rotations

Node 30 is left-heavy by 2 — but its left child 10 leans the other way (to the right, toward 20). That "zig-zag" can't be fixed by one rotation; the tall part is in the middle. So you straighten it first: rotate the child (10) left, turning the zig-zag into a straight left-left lean — and then do the single right rotation on 30.

 

RL is this in a mirror: rotate the right child right to straighten, then rotate the node left. That's all four cases — two of them just reflections of the other two.

That's the whole trick behind self-balancing: after each change, check the balance factor and, if it's ±2, apply the matching single or double rotation — O(1) work that keeps the tree O(log n) tall forever.
