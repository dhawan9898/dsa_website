Words That Share A Start, Share A Path

Trie (Prefix Tree)

A tree where every edge is a letter, so following a path spells a word — the structure behind autocomplete and spell-check.

Overview Playground

The idea, in one picture

Think of an index at the back of a book, but nested. To find every word starting with ca, you'd flip to the c section, then the ca sub-section, and there they all are — cat, car, can — grouped together because they share a beginning. A trie (say "try", from retrieval) is exactly that idea as a tree.

Each edge is labelled with a single letter, and each node is the prefix you've spelled by walking down from the root. So the root is the empty string; step down a c edge and you're at "c"; step down an a and you're at "ca". Words that begin the same way walk the same path until they differ — that shared path is the whole trick.

Two rules. (1) A word is stored by the path of its letters from the root, not by any value inside a node. (2) Because "ca" is a prefix of "cat" but not a word by itself, each node carries a small end-of-word flag — drawn here as a green ring — that says "a real word ends right here."

Why it's fast

lookup depends on the word, not the dictionary

To check whether a word of length L is in the trie, you walk L edges — one per letter — and then look at the end-of-word flag. That's it. It does not matter whether the trie holds ten words or ten million: the work is O(L), set only by the length of the word you're looking up. A hash table can match that on a full-word lookup, but a trie also answers something a hash can't cheaply: "give me every word starting with ca." — just walk to the "ca" node and read everything below it.

The catch is memory. Every distinct prefix character is its own node. A trie trades space for speed: many pointers (one slot per possible next letter at each node), most of them empty. That's why real implementations use compressed or map-backed nodes — but the shape you see here is the honest mental model.

costs and where tries show up
OperationWhat it doesTime

SearchWalk the letters, check end-of-wordO(L)
Starts-with (prefix)Walk to the prefix node; a match means it existsO(L)
DeleteUnmark end-of-word; prune now-unused nodesO(L)

L is the length of the word — never the number of words stored. That independence from dictionary size is the trie's signature.

Where you'll meet tries. Autocomplete and search suggestions (walk to what's typed, list the subtree); spell-checkers and word games (is this a valid word? is it a prefix of one?); IP routing tables and phone-number prefix matching; and any "type-ahead" that has to answer prefix questions faster than scanning a list.
