Double-Ended Queue

Deque

A queue open at both ends: you can add and remove at the front or the rear, whichever you need.

The idea, in one picture

Deque (say "deck") is short for double-ended queue. Picture a row of train cars you can couple or uncouple from either end — the left or the right. A plain queue makes you join at the back and leave from the front; a deque drops that restriction, so any of four moves is allowed.

Four moves. Push front and push rear add a value at the left or right end; pop front and pop rear remove from the left or right end. (Many libraries also offer peek-front and peek-rear to look without removing.)

Because it can do everything a stack and a queue can, a deque is a kind of Swiss-army container: restrict it to one end and it behaves as a stack (LIFO); push at one end and pop at the other and it's a queue (FIFO).

How it actually works

a wrapping array, moving both ways

The cleanest build reuses the circular queue's trick — a fixed array with indices that wrap using % MAX — but now the front index can move backwards too, not just rear forwards. Keep front and a size count; the rear is always (front + size - 1) % MAX.

  • Push rear: write at (front + size) % MAX, then size++ — same as the circular queue.
  • Push front: step front back one with a wrap, front = (front - 1 + MAX) % MAX, write there, then size++. (The + MAX keeps the number non-negative before the %.)
  • Pop front / pop rear: read the end slot, then shrink from that side.

Every move is a couple of index steps — no shifting, no walking — so all four are O(1). Try each end in the visualizer:

Ends only. Like its cousins, a deque is fast precisely because it only promises the two ends. There's no efficient "insert in the middle" — reach for a different structure if you need that.

Where you'll meet deques. The classic use is the sliding-window maximum problem, where a deque of indices keeps a window's largest value in O(1); also undo/redo with a bounded history (drop the oldest from one end while pushing new work on the other), work-stealing schedulers (a thread pushes and pops its own end while idle threads steal from the far end), and a browser's combined back/forward history. Backed by a doubly linked list instead of an array, a deque needs no fixed capacity at all.
